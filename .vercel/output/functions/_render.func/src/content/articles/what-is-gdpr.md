GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — is the most significant data privacy law in the world. It reshaped how organizations collect, store, process, and protect personal data. Even if you're not in Europe, GDPR likely affects you.

## What it is

GDPR is a regulation adopted by the European Union that took effect on **May 25, 2018**. It governs the processing of personal data of individuals in the EU and EEA (European Economic Area), regardless of where the processing happens.

That last part is critical: if you have customers in the EU, GDPR applies to you, even if you're based in the US, India, or anywhere else.

## Key principles

**Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency** — You must have a legal basis for processing personal data, and you must be clear about what you're doing with it.

**Purpose limitation** — Collect data for specific, explicit purposes. Don't collect it for one thing and use it for something else.

**Data minimization** — Only collect what you actually need. Don't hoard data "just in case."

**Accuracy** — Keep personal data accurate and up to date.

**Storage limitation** — Don't keep data longer than necessary.

**Integrity and confidentiality** — Protect data with appropriate security measures. This is where GDPR intersects most directly with cybersecurity.

**Accountability** — You must be able to demonstrate compliance. It's not enough to be compliant — you must prove it.

## Your rights (as an individual)

GDPR gives individuals significant control over their data:

- **Right to access** — You can ask any organization what data they hold about you, and they must provide it (typically within 30 days)
- **Right to rectification** — Correct inaccurate data
- **Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten")** — Request deletion of your data
- **Right to restrict processing** — Limit how your data is used
- **Right to data portability** — Get your data in a machine-readable format and transfer it to another service
- **Right to object** — Object to certain types of processing, including direct marketing
- **Rights related to automated decision-making** — Challenge decisions made purely by algorithms

## The security requirement

Article 32 of GDPR mandates "appropriate technical and organizational measures" to protect data. This includes:

- Encryption of personal data
- Ability to ensure ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience
- Ability to restore access to data in a timely manner after an incident
- Regular testing and evaluation of security measures

"Appropriate" scales with risk. A hospital holding medical records needs stronger protections than a newsletter signup form.

## Breach notification

GDPR introduced mandatory breach notification:

- **To the supervisory authority**: Within **72 hours** of becoming aware of a breach, if it poses a risk to individuals
- **To affected individuals**: "Without undue delay" if the breach poses a high risk to their rights and freedoms

This 72-hour window is tight and requires organizations to have incident response capabilities ready before a breach occurs.

## Penalties

GDPR enforcement is serious:

- Up to **€20 million or 4% of global annual turnover** (whichever is higher) for the most serious violations
- Up to **€10 million or 2% of global annual turnover** for lesser violations

Major fines have already been issued: Amazon (€746 million), Meta (€1.2 billion), Google (various), and many others. While some fines have been reduced on appeal, the enforcement trend is toward stricter, larger penalties.

## Impact beyond Europe

GDPR started a global wave of privacy legislation:

- **California (CCPA/CPRA)** — US state-level equivalent
- **Brazil (LGPD)** — Brazil's comprehensive data protection law
- **India (DPDP Act)** — India's digital personal data protection framework
- **China (PIPL)** — China's personal information protection law

GDPR became the template. Many organizations apply GDPR-level protections globally because it's simpler than maintaining different standards for different regions.

## The bottom line

GDPR fundamentally changed the relationship between organizations and personal data. It established that personal data belongs to the individual, not the organization that collects it. Organizations are stewards, not owners.

For cybersecurity professionals, GDPR provides regulatory teeth to security mandates. It's no longer just best practice to encrypt data and have breach notification plans — it's the law, with significant financial consequences for non-compliance.
