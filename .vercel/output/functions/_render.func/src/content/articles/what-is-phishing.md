Phishing is the most common attack in cybersecurity. Not the most sophisticated. Not the most damaging. Just the most effective, because it targets the one vulnerability that can't be patched: human nature.

## What it looks like

You get an email that looks like it's from your bank. The logo is right. The formatting is convincing. It says there's been suspicious activity on your account and you need to verify your identity — click this link now.

The link goes to a website that looks exactly like your bank's login page. You enter your username and password. The site says "verification failed, please try again." You go to the real bank site and everything works fine. You think nothing of it.

Meanwhile, the attacker now has your banking credentials.

That's phishing. It works because it exploits trust, urgency, and habit.

## The mechanics

**Step 1: Bait creation.** The attacker creates a convincing lure — an email, text message (that's called **smishing**), or phone call (**vishing**) that appears to come from a trusted source. Banks, tech companies, your employer, shipping services — whoever you're likely to trust and act on quickly.

**Step 2: Delivery.** The lure is sent en masse (for broad phishing campaigns) or to a specific target (for **spear phishing**).

**Step 3: Action.** The victim clicks a link, opens an attachment, or enters information on a fake site.

**Step 4: Exploitation.** The attacker uses the stolen information — credentials, credit card numbers, personal data — for financial fraud, account takeover, or as a foothold for deeper attacks.

## Why it works so well

Phishing succeeds because it exploits real psychological tendencies:

- **Urgency** — "Your account will be closed in 24 hours!" People act fast when they feel time pressure.
- **Authority** — "This is the IT department. We need you to verify your credentials." People comply with perceived authority.
- **Familiarity** — It looks like an email from Amazon, Microsoft, or Netflix — brands you interact with constantly.
- **Fear** — "Unauthorized access detected." Fear triggers quick, emotional responses.
- **Curiosity** — "You've received a document for review." People click.

The best phishing emails have no spelling errors, use correct branding, and create a plausible scenario. The days of obvious "Nigerian prince" scams are long gone.

## Real-world impact

- Over **90% of successful cyber attacks** start with a phishing email
- Business Email Compromise (BEC) — a targeted form of phishing — caused over **$2.9 billion in losses** in 2023 alone according to the FBI
- Phishing is the initial access vector for most ransomware attacks

## Variations

- **Spear phishing** — Highly targeted at specific individuals, using personal information to build credibility (e.g., referencing your actual colleagues, projects, or recent activities)
- **Whale phishing** — Targeting high-value individuals like CEOs, CFOs, or executives with access to sensitive systems or financial authority
- **Clone phishing** — Copying a legitimate email the victim has already received and replacing links or attachments with malicious ones
- **Vishing** — Voice phishing over phone calls, often using AI-generated voice to impersonate someone the victim knows
- **Smishing** — SMS/text message phishing, increasingly common as people trust text messages more than email

## How to spot it (most of the time)

- **Check the sender address carefully** — `support@amaz0n-security.com` is not Amazon
- **Hover over links before clicking** — See where they actually go
- **Be skeptical of urgency** — Legitimate services don't threaten imminent account closure
- **Watch for unusual requests** — Your bank will never ask you to "verify" your password via email
- **Look for generic greetings** — "Dear Customer" instead of your actual name (though spear phishing may use your real name)
- **When in doubt, go directly** — Open a new browser tab and navigate to the site manually instead of clicking the email link

## The organizations' problem

For companies, phishing is an ongoing battle. Technical defenses (email filtering, link scanning, attachment sandboxing) catch a lot, but not everything. Security awareness training helps, but humans forget, get busy, and make mistakes.

The most effective approach combines technical controls, regular simulated phishing exercises (sending fake phishing emails to test employees), and a culture where people report suspicious emails without fear of embarrassment.

## The bottom line

Phishing works because it bypasses all your technical defenses and goes straight to the human. No firewall stops you from clicking a link you believe is legitimate. No antivirus prevents you from entering your password on a convincing fake site.

The best defense is a combination of healthy skepticism, verification habits, and technical controls that add layers of protection. But perfect defense doesn't exist — which is why phishing remains the #1 attack vector year after year.
