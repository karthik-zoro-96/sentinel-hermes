The dark web generates a lot of fear and confusion. Most of what people think they know about it comes from movies and news headlines. Let's separate reality from mythology.

## First, some terminology

**Surface web** — What you use every day. Websites indexed by Google, Bing, etc. Facebook, Wikipedia, news sites. This is roughly 4-10% of the total content on the internet.

**Deep web** — Everything not indexed by search engines. Your email inbox, online banking portal, private databases, medical records, corporate internal sites. This is the vast majority of the internet. It's not sinister — it's just content behind login walls and not meant to be publicly indexed.

**Dark web** — A small subset of the deep web that requires specific software to access. The most common access method is **Tor** (The Onion Router). Dark web sites use the `.onion` domain and are not accessible through regular browsers.

## How Tor works

Tor routes your traffic through multiple volunteer-operated servers (nodes), encrypting it in layers (hence "onion"). Each node only knows the previous and next hop, so no single node knows both who you are and what you're accessing.

This provides **anonymity** — not perfect anonymity, but meaningful privacy. It's the same technology used by journalists communicating with sources, activists in authoritarian regimes, and ordinary people who don't want their browsing tracked.

## What's actually on the dark web

**Legitimate uses:**
- Journalism and whistleblowing (SecureDrop, used by major news organizations)
- Privacy-conscious communication
- Accessing information in censored regions
- Research and education

**Illicit marketplaces:**
- Stolen data (credit cards, credentials, personal information)
- Drugs
- Counterfeit documents
- Hacking tools and services
- Ransomware-as-a-service offerings

**Other content:**
- Forums discussing hacking, security research, and various ideologies
- Leaked databases from breaches (often available for free)
- Scam sites (a significant portion of dark web marketplaces are scams)

## The reality check

The dark web is not the lawless digital wasteland it's made out to be:

- **Law enforcement actively operates there.** The FBI, Europol, and other agencies have taken down major marketplaces (Silk Road, AlphaBay, Hydra) and arrested their operators.
- **Many marketplaces are scams.** Exit scams (where marketplace operators disappear with users' cryptocurrency) are common.
- **It's smaller than you think.** The total number of active `.onion` sites is in the tens of thousands — tiny compared to the surface web.
- **Accessing it is not illegal** in most jurisdictions. Using Tor is legal. What you do there may not be.

## Why security professionals care

The dark web is where stolen data ends up. After a breach, compromised credentials, credit card numbers, and personal information are sold or traded on dark web marketplaces. **Dark web monitoring** — scanning these marketplaces for your organization's data — is a common security service.

If your credentials appear on the dark web, it means one of the services you use was breached. It's a signal to change passwords and enable MFA.

## The bottom line

The dark web is a tool. Like any tool, it can be used for good or ill. It provides genuine privacy benefits for people who need them, and it also hosts criminal marketplaces. Understanding it demystifies it — it's not magic, it's not ungovernable, and it's not something the average person needs to lose sleep over.

What matters more for most people: use unique passwords, enable MFA, and monitor whether your credentials have appeared in known breaches (haveibeenpwned.com). That does more for your security than worrying about the dark web.
