A data breach is when sensitive, protected, or confidential data is accessed, copied, transmitted, or used by someone who isn't authorized to do so. It's the security incident that most people care about — the one where personal information ends up in the wrong hands.

## What gets breached

- **Personal identifiable information (PII)** — Names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, social security numbers, dates of birth
- **Financial data** — Credit card numbers, bank account details, transaction records
- **Credentials** — Usernames and passwords (especially when passwords are stored improperly instead of hashed)
- **Health data** — Medical records, insurance information, prescription details
- **Intellectual property** — Trade secrets, source code, proprietary designs
- **Government data** — Classified information, personnel records, surveillance data

## How breaches happen

Breach causes generally fall into a few categories:

**External attacks (40-50%)** — Hackers exploiting vulnerabilities, phishing employees, or using stolen credentials to access systems directly.

**Human error (20-30%)** — Misconfigured databases accidentally exposed to the internet, emails sent to the wrong recipient, lost devices, unsecured cloud storage buckets. Not malicious, but equally damaging.

**Insider threats (10-15%)** — Employees or contractors who steal data deliberately, often for financial gain or as they leave an organization.

**Third-party/ supply chain (10-15%)** — A vendor or partner with access to your systems is compromised, and the attacker uses that access to reach you.

**Physical theft** — Stolen laptops, USB drives, or paper records. Still happens more than you'd think.

## What happens to stolen data

Stolen data typically ends up in one or more of these places:

- **Sold on dark web marketplaces** — Credit card numbers, credentials, and personal data are commodities with set prices. Full identity packages ("fullz") sell for $10-100+ depending on what's included and how fresh the data is.
- **Used for account takeover** — Stolen credentials are tested against banking, email, and shopping sites. People who reuse passwords are especially vulnerable.
- **Used for identity theft** — Opening credit accounts, filing fraudulent tax returns, or obtaining medical care under your name.
- **Used for further attacks** — Personal information enables highly targeted spear phishing and social engineering.
- **Held for ransom** — Threatened with public release unless the victim pays.

## The cost

According to IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report (2024):

- Average cost: **$4.88 million** per breach globally
- Average time to identify and contain: **277 days**
- Breaches involving stolen/compromised credentials took longest to identify (average 328 days)

Costs include: investigation, legal fees, regulatory fines, customer notification, credit monitoring services, lost business, and reputation damage.

## Major breaches

- **Yahoo (2013)** — 3 billion accounts compromised. Took 3 years to disclose. Impacted the Verizon acquisition price by $350 million.
- **Equifax (2017)** — 147 million people's personal data, including social security numbers. Resulted in a $700 million settlement.
- **Marriott (2018)** — 500 million guest records including passport numbers. Demonstrated the risk of M&A inheriting breached infrastructure.
- **T-Mobile (multiple, 2018-2023)** — Tens of millions of customer records compromised across multiple incidents.
- **MOVEit (2023)** — A single vulnerability in a file transfer tool led to breaches at over 2,500 organizations and 65 million+ individuals.

## What you can do (as an individual)

You can't prevent a company from being breached. But you can limit the impact:

- **Use unique passwords for every service** — A password manager makes this practical
- **Enable MFA everywhere** — Even if your password leaks, your accounts stay protected
- **Monitor your accounts** — Check bank and credit card statements regularly
- **Freeze your credit** — Prevents new accounts from being opened in your name
- **Be skeptical of unexpected communications** — Breach notifications, password resets, and verification requests can all be phishing attempts piggybacking on real breaches
- **Check haveibeenpwned.com** — See if your email has appeared in known breaches

## What organizations should do

- **Encrypt sensitive data at rest and in transit** — Encrypted data is much less useful to attackers
- **Limit data collection** — Don't store what you don't need. You can't breach data you never collected
- **Implement access controls** — Not everyone needs access to sensitive data
- **Monitor and log access** — Know who's accessing what
- **Have an incident response plan** — Know who to call and what to do before it happens
- **Compliance isn't optional** — Regulations like GDPR, HIPAA, and state privacy laws mandate how breaches must be handled and notified

## The bottom line

Data breaches are inevitable when data exists. The question isn't "will a breach happen" but "what happens when it does." Organizations that minimize the data they collect, encrypt what they keep, and respond quickly when things go wrong are in a fundamentally different position than those that hoard data and hope for the best.

For individuals, unique passwords and MFA are your best personal defense — because you can't control whether a company's security is good enough, but you can make sure one breach doesn't cascade into compromised accounts everywhere.
