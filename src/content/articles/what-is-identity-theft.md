Identity theft is when someone uses your personal information — without your permission — to commit fraud or other crimes. It's one of the most common and most personally devastating forms of cybercrime. Unlike a credit card number that can be replaced, your identity is yours and only yours.

## How it happens

**Data breaches** — Your information is stolen in a breach of a company you've entrusted with your data. Social security numbers, dates of birth, addresses, account numbers.

**Phishing and social engineering** — You're tricked into providing personal information directly.

**Mail theft and dumpster diving** — Old-school but still effective. Pre-approved credit card offers, tax documents, medical statements.

**Public records and OSINT** — Much of your personal information is publicly available: property records, court filings, voter registration, social media. Attackers piece together profiles from multiple sources.

**Synthetic identity theft** — The attacker combines real and fake information to create a new identity. For example, using a real social security number (often a child's or deceased person's) with a fake name and address. This is harder to detect because the identity doesn't match any single real person.

## What attackers do with stolen identities

- **Open financial accounts** — Credit cards, loans, bank accounts in your name
- **File fraudulent tax returns** — Claim your refund before you file
- **Obtain medical care** — Using your insurance, creating medical records that become yours
- **Commit crimes** — Give your name to police when arrested
- **Rent property** — Establish residency under your name
- **Employment** — Work under your social security number

## The impact

Identity theft is uniquely personal. Victims often spend **months or years** cleaning up the damage: disputing fraudulent accounts, repairing credit, communicating with law courts and law enforcement. The FTC estimates that identity theft costs victims an average of 200+ hours of recovery time.

Financial losses vary widely:
- Direct financial theft from accounts
- Legal fees for resolving fraudulent activities
- Lost wages from time spent on recovery
- Emotional and psychological toll (anxiety, loss of trust, feeling of violation)

## Types

**Financial identity theft** — The most common. Using your information for financial gain.

**Medical identity theft** — Using your identity to obtain medical services. Particularly dangerous because it can corrupt your medical records with incorrect information (blood type, allergies, diagnoses) that could lead to medical errors in the future.

**Criminal identity theft** — Someone gives your name and information when arrested. You may not discover this until a background check or a warrant is issued in your name.

**Child identity theft** — Children's social security numbers are valuable because they're often unused and won't be checked for years. A child may not discover their identity was stolen until they apply for their first loan or job.

## How to protect yourself

- **Freeze your credit** — The single most effective step. Prevents new accounts from being opened in your name. Free to do with all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). Unfreeze temporarily when you need to apply for credit.
- **Use unique passwords and MFA** — Limits the damage if one service is breached
- **Monitor accounts** — Check bank, credit card, and insurance statements regularly
- **File taxes early** — Reduces the window for fraudulent returns
- **Shred sensitive documents** — Physical security still matters
- **Be careful what you share** — Social media oversharing provides material for social engineering and identity construction
- **Check your credit reports** — You're entitled to free weekly reports from all three bureaus

## If you're a victim

1. **File a report with the FTC** at IdentityTheft.gov (US)
2. **File a police report**
3. **Contact your financial institutions** — Close compromised accounts
4. **Place a fraud alert or credit freeze** with all three bureaus
5. **Dispute fraudulent accounts and charges** in writing
6. **Document everything** — Keep records of all communications and actions

## The bottom line

Identity theft is personal in a way that other cybercrimes aren't. It's not just about money — it's about someone wearing your life like a costume. Prevention is far easier than recovery. Freeze your credit, use MFA, monitor your accounts, and be thoughtful about what personal information you share and where.

The uncomfortable truth: much of your personal information is already out there from past breaches. The question isn't whether your data has been compromised — it probably has — but whether you've taken steps to make it harder for someone to profit from it.
